# Third Opinion: Adjudication and Additions

## A review of `RESEARCH.md` *and* `RESEARCH-COUNTERPOINTS.md`

**Status:** Independent review — reconciles the two prior documents and adds material neither contains
**Date:** 2026-08-03
**Method:** Independent literature and practitioner search, deliberately weighted toward (a) independent/adversarial evaluation, (b) 2026 preprints that postdate both prior documents, and (c) primary sources. Vendor material is cited only where it reports a *measurement* or an *architectural reversal*, and is labelled as vendor-reported every time.
**Scope note:** `RESEARCH-COUNTERPOINTS.md` already exists in this directory. This document does not repeat it. Where I agree with it I say so in one line and move on; where I think it is wrong I argue.

---

## 0. What this document is for

The two existing documents stage a clean argument:

- **`RESEARCH.md`** proposes a layered, provenance-heavy, database-backed system with a hybrid retrieval pipeline.
- **`RESEARCH-COUNTERPOINTS.md`** argues that is over-built 2024-shaped RAG, and proposes markdown files + git + ripgrep + an agentic tool loop, shipped in days.

Both are defensible. **Neither is wrong, and that is the actual finding.** The evidence that postdates both documents says the correct answer *changes as a function of how long the brain has been running*, which means the interesting decision is not "which architecture" but "what triggers the transition, and how will you know you've hit it."

That is §1 below and it is the single most important thing in this document.

Sections 2–9 are additions neither document contains. Section 10 is where I think `RESEARCH-COUNTERPOINTS.md` is materially wrong. Section 11 is a consolidated change list.

## 1. The two documents are both right, at different times

### 1.1 The evidence

*Ground Truth First: A Longitudinal Evaluation Instrument for Agent Memory, and the Tenure Crossover in Memory-Architecture Rankings* ([arXiv 2607.21962](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21962v1), July 2026) is the most directly relevant paper I found, and it postdates both prior documents.

The authors inverted the usual benchmark pipeline. Instead of generating conversations and extracting a gold answer key afterwards — the procedure that produced LoCoMo's 6.4% error rate — they emit facts *first*, from a seeded life-script sampler, each carrying a validity interval, a **volatility class**, and a source channel. Text is generated from the facts. Gold answers are mechanically instantiated rather than annotated. Roughly 380 questions across 15 types.

The headline result:

| Architecture | ~3 weeks tenure | ~9 weeks tenure |
|---|---:|---:|
| Budget-conscious curated map (≈ the `COUNTERPOINTS` proposal) | 96% recall | **72%** |
| Provenance-typed graph (≈ the `RESEARCH.md` proposal) | lower | **90%** |

The ranking **inverts**, and the inversion held across all six test users (p = 0.031).

Two supporting findings from the same paper:

- **Write-stage quality dominates.** Weakly-written facts failed downstream retrieval at **24%** versus **2%** for well-written ones. The write path, not the retrieval path, is where accuracy is decided.
- **Full-rendered-history baselines tied the best memory systems at short horizons**, and showed no judge-independent advantage at nine weeks — at roughly **twice the read cost**.

**Honest caveats, because they matter here:** n = 6 synthetic users, one research group, an unreplicated preprint, and a synthetic corpus generated by the same team that defines the scoring. p = 0.031 on six subjects is a weak signal. I am treating this as *directional*, not settled. But it is the only evidence I found that varies tenure as an independent variable, and every prior benchmark holds tenure fixed — which means every prior benchmark is structurally incapable of detecting this effect.

### 1.2 Why this reframes both documents

`RESEARCH-COUNTERPOINTS.md` §13 proposes shipping in week one with markdown + git + ripgrep. On this evidence that is **correct for the first month and progressively wrong after that**, for a mechanical reason: a curated map degrades through *eviction* — as the corpus grows, curation either falls behind or starts discarding things, and recall decays. It does not degrade because grep is bad. It degrades because curation does not scale sublinearly with corpus size and human attention does not scale at all.

`RESEARCH.md` is the architecture that wins at nine weeks and loses on time-to-first-value. Its problem is not that it is wrong; it is that it asks for Phases 0–3 of engineering before a user notices anything, and the payoff it is designed for does not arrive until the corpus is large enough for the payoff to exist.

### 1.3 What I would actually do

Adopt `COUNTERPOINTS`' Phase 0.5 — **but only if you simultaneously commit to the instrumentation that detects the crossover**, and write down the trigger before you start. Otherwise you will build the cheap thing, it will work beautifully for six weeks, it will quietly start losing things, and you will not notice, because *nothing in either document measures recall against a growing corpus*.

Concretely, add to Phase 0.5:

1. **A fixed golden set, re-run weekly, unchanged.** Not regenerated, not extended — the same questions, forever. The signal you are looking for is the *slope* of accuracy over corpus size, not its level.
2. **A written migration trigger** in `docs/decisions/`, of the form: *"when golden-set recall drops below X% or when the weekly slope is negative for N consecutive weeks, we build the index."* `RESEARCH.md` §12 already contemplates a migration trigger for PostgreSQL; it just never defines one, and it defines it on the wrong axis (concurrency) rather than the axis the evidence implicates (tenure).
3. **Volatility class as a required write-time field.** See §4 below — this is the field that determines *how* the curated map degrades, and neither document has it.

This is a genuine reconciliation, not a split-the-difference. It says: build the cheap thing, and pre-commit to the expensive thing on a measured trigger. The migration-boundary discipline in `RESEARCH.md` §16.3 is exactly what makes it safe to defer, and it is already written.

## 2. Both documents evaluate the wrong object

This is my second-strongest disagreement, and it applies to `RESEARCH.md` §15 and `COUNTERPOINTS` §4.1 equally.

`COUNTERPOINTS` §4.1 proposes a **memory-off control arm** — run every eval case with and without the brain, report the delta — and calls it "the only number that answers 'is this system worth its cost.'" I agree it is necessary. I think it is insufficient, and I think as a sole gate it will actively mislead you.

*MEMPROBE: Probing Long-Term Agent Memory via Hidden User-State Recovery* ([arXiv 2606.24595](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24595v1)) sets up 50 simulated users with 31 hidden state dimensions each (1,550 recovery targets), lets agents assist those users across task trajectories, then attempts to reconstruct the user-state bank *from the memory the agent left behind*.

Findings:

- **Task completion rates nearly saturated even for systems with no memory at all.**
- Category-balanced state recovery sat at roughly **0.6**, and dropped further under top-k retrieval.
- The authors' conclusion: successful assistance and recoverable memory are **distinct capabilities**.

The implication for the memory-off control arm is direct and uncomfortable: **on most tasks it will show approximately no delta**, because tasks are completable without memory. If that is your gate, you will conclude the brain is not worth building — and you will be measuring the wrong thing. The value of a second brain is not that it raises task success this week; it is that the store is a faithful, auditable model of what you actually decided, which is a property of the *store*, not of any downstream task.

**Addition to the evaluation plan (both documents):** a **state-recovery probe**. Maintain a private list of things that are true about you, your projects, and your decisions — facts you have definitely told the system. Periodically ask the store to reconstruct them, cold, with no conversational context. Score recovery. This is cheap for a single user (a hand-written YAML file of 50–100 facts), it is the direct audit rather than the indirect behavioral one, and it is the number that actually degrades when the curated map starts evicting.

Run **both**: memory-off delta answers "is this earning its token cost," state-recovery answers "is this still faithful." They fail in different directions and neither substitutes for the other.

## 3. Failure attribution before optimization

`RESEARCH.md` §11 spends ~90 lines on retrieval architecture. `COUNTERPOINTS` §2 spends ~40 arguing it is the wrong shape. Both are arguing about a component whose contribution to end-to-end failure neither has measured.

Two 2026 preprints decompose this:

- *A-TMA: Decoupling State-Aware Memory Failures in Long-Term Agent Memory* ([arXiv 2607.01935](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.01935)) splits memory failure into **retention** (it was never stored, or was lost), **retrieval** (it exists but wasn't found), **relevance** (found, but wrong for the context), and **consistency** (conflicting facts stored simultaneously). The argument is that these require different fixes and that effort should be concentrated on whichever dominates *your* corpus — which is not knowable a priori.
- The Zep/Graphiti line of work makes the same point sharply: a graph can correctly preserve both the old and the new fact, retrieval can still select the wrong one, and the generator can still fail to decide which one answers the query. **Representation does not guarantee correctness.**

`Ground Truth First`'s 24%-vs-2% write-quality finding suggests that for a personal brain, **retention/write-quality dominates**, not retrieval. If that holds for you, then the entire §11.3 hybrid pipeline debate — and `COUNTERPOINTS`' rebuttal to it — is an argument about the second-largest term.

**Addition:** before optimizing anything, every golden-set failure gets tagged with one of the four categories. That is a five-minute-per-failure human judgment for the first fifty failures, and it tells you which of the two prior documents' recommendations you should actually implement first. Neither document has a failure taxonomy at all; both jump straight to remedies.

## 4. The missing field: volatility class

`RESEARCH.md` §8.3 requires fourteen metadata fields on every memory revision. `COUNTERPOINTS` §6.2 correctly says that is too many for a one-sentence memory and proposes cutting to five.

I agree it is too many. I also think **the single most load-bearing field is absent from all fourteen**.

`RESEARCH.md` §11.4 contains what `COUNTERPOINTS` rightly calls its best line: *"Decay should affect activation, not truth."* But it then applies decay as a global ranking signal. That is wrong in a way that a personal brain will feel immediately, because facts about a person have wildly different half-lives:

| Fact | Half-life | Correct decay behavior |
|---|---|---|
| "I was born in March" | permanent | none, ever |
| "I prefer concise summaries" | years | very slow, revisit on contradiction |
| "I'm using Postgres for this project" | months | decay fast; re-confirm aggressively |
| "I'm blocked on the auth bug" | days | expire by default |

A single decay curve, or a single recency boost, is guaranteed to be wrong for three of these four. `Ground Truth First` bakes **volatility class** into fact generation for exactly this reason, and the "Consolidation Problem" analysis gives the concrete failure it produces: an assistant that *"confidently tells a customer they're still on Postgres, six weeks after they migrated to MySQL"* — which is a consolidation failure, not a retrieval failure, and no amount of reranking fixes it.

**Recommendation:** replace `RESEARCH.md`'s fourteen required fields with six, one of which is new:

1. `id`
2. `type` (episodic / semantic / preference / procedural / task)
3. `provenance_class` (categorical — see §10.3 below)
4. `volatility` (**new**: `immutable` / `slow` / `volatile` / `ephemeral`)
5. `valid_from`
6. `evidence` (source pointer)

Everything else optional. `volatility` determines the decay curve, the re-confirmation cadence, and the default expiry — three behaviors that both documents currently derive from nothing.

## 5. Procedural memory is prompt optimization, and neither document treats it that way

`RESEARCH.md` §10.3 handles agent-authored lessons with a review queue. `COUNTERPOINTS` §7 adds that unreviewed memories should expire. Both are *governance* answers. Neither notices that procedural memory is the one memory type that has a **measurable objective function**, and therefore the one that should be optimized rather than curated.

This is a live research area both documents miss entirely:

- **ACE (Agentic Context Engineering)** ([arXiv 2510.04618](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04618), Stanford / SambaNova / UC Berkeley) treats the instruction context as an evolving *playbook* maintained by a Generator / Reflector / Curator split, reporting +10.6% on agent tasks and +8.6% on domain benchmarks over GEPA and in-context-learning baselines, with 82–92% lower adaptation latency.
- **GEPA** ([repo](https://github.com/gepa-ai/gepa), integrated into DSPy) evolves prompts by reflecting on execution traces — reasoning paths, tool outputs, compiler errors — rather than on scalar rewards.

The finding from ACE that matters most for this project is its diagnosis of **context collapse**: methods that optimize for brevity by *repeatedly rewriting the whole context* lose detail monotonically. ACE's fix is **delta updates** — incremental modifications that never ask the model to regenerate the entire artifact.

This is a direct, mechanistic strike on `RESEARCH.md` §10.2, which specifies a consolidation cascade of `raw events → session summaries → topic summaries → profile views`. §10.2 already warns "avoid recursively summarizing summaries without checking original evidence," which is the right instinct — ACE supplies the mechanism for *why* and a concrete alternative. **Consolidation should append and restructure, not regenerate.** A consolidation step that rewrites `AGENTS.md` or a skill file wholesale is the exact operation ACE identifies as lossy.

**Additions:**

1. Procedural memories get a **held-out task set**. A lesson that does not improve measured task outcomes on held-out cases does not get promoted, regardless of how sensible it reads. This is stronger than a human review queue and cheaper to run.
2. **Consolidation is delta-only.** No pipeline step may regenerate a curated artifact from scratch. Enforceable as a diff-size check in CI.
3. Treat `AGENTS.md` and skills as **optimizable artifacts with a train/test split**, not as documentation. This is the part of the brain most likely to produce compounding value, and it is the part both documents treat most casually.

## 6. Write triggers: the "explicit-first" recommendation has a failure mode neither document names

`COUNTERPOINTS` §8 correctly identifies that `RESEARCH.md` never says *what invokes* the write pipeline, and proposes: explicit-first, session-batch second, scheduled consolidation third, never per-turn. The cost/noise/poisoning analysis is right.

The gap: **explicit-only writes have near-zero capture rate.** The dominant failure mode of a personal knowledge system is not poisoning and not noise — it is **emptiness**. Every personal-knowledge-management tool in history has died of disuse, not of corruption. A brain that only remembers what you consciously chose to tell it is a notes app, and you already have one.

The sleep-time-compute literature gives the shape of the fix and some numbers: moving non-latency-critical work to a background path with a narrower toolset and a cheaper model reduces test-time compute for equivalent accuracy by roughly **5×**, and amortized across related queries drops per-query cost around **2.5×** ([Letta, arXiv 2504.13171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13171); pattern summarized in [Jatin Bansal's writeup](https://jatinbansal.com/ai-engineering/sleep-time-compute/)).

**Recommended trigger policy** — `COUNTERPOINTS` §8 plus its own §7.2 expiry, combined:

| Path | Trigger | Default state | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Explicit | user says "remember this" | `confirmed` | ~0 |
| Session-end batch | end of session, background, cheap model | `proposed`, expires in 30 days | amortized |
| Scheduled consolidation | nightly/weekly, background | `proposed`, delta-only | amortized |
| Per-turn | never | — | — |

The key move is that **the batch path writes freely but everything it writes is on a decay timer.** Capture rate goes up, poisoning exposure stays bounded, and the accretion dynamic that degrades memory systems over months is inverted — unconfirmed, never-cited memories lapse on their own. You get the capture of implicit writes with the trust profile of explicit ones.

The one thing this requires that neither document specifies: **a review surface that costs one keystroke.** If reviewing proposals is a chore, they will all expire and you are back to explicit-only. Budget for this as a real UI, not a CLI subcommand.

## 7. Bitemporality is under-specified in a way that is known to fail

`RESEARCH.md` §8.4 specifies valid-time and transaction-time and stops. `COUNTERPOINTS` §12 correctly notes this is substantially a re-implementation of Graphiti/Zep's bitemporal edge model and asks that the reimplementation be acknowledged. I agree, and there is a further problem neither raises.

*TOKI: A Bitemporal Operator Algebra for Contradiction Resolution in LLM-Agent Persistent Memory* ([arXiv 2606.06240](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.06240)) identifies the gap directly: **naive bitemporal modeling leaves "which version of this fact is true now" ambiguous when contradictory facts have overlapping validity intervals.** Storing two timestamps does not give you a resolution rule; it gives you the data on which a resolution rule would operate. `RESEARCH.md` §8.4 stores the data and never writes the rule.

Compounding this, entity resolution — deciding that two references denote the same thing — is described as an **unsolved problem in production** in the Graphiti/Zep line of work, with resolution quality depending entirely on the extraction model. So the two mechanisms `RESEARCH.md` relies on for temporal correctness (bitemporal intervals + supersession links) both have known, documented failure modes at exactly the point where they are load-bearing.

**Additions:**

1. **Write the precedence policy as code, before Phase 1.** A total order, in `docs/decisions/`. Something like: direct user statement > authoritative document > agent inference; then later transaction time wins; then narrower valid-time interval wins. It will be wrong in cases; a written wrong rule is auditable and a missing rule is not.
2. **Make "return both the current and the superseded fact" illegal at the retrieval boundary by default.** The consolidation-failure literature is unanimous that handing a model two contradictory facts about one entity produces incoherent output *even from strong models*. If the retriever cannot determine precedence, it should return the fact plus an explicit `unresolved_conflict` marker — not both facts silently.
3. Do not attempt automatic entity resolution in the MVP. For a single user, entity aliasing is a hand-maintained list of maybe 200 entries and it will beat a model at it.

## 8. Cache economics: `COUNTERPOINTS` §3 is right, and the real finding is worse than it says

`COUNTERPOINTS` §3 argues that a context builder injecting fresh memories near the top of the prompt destroys the prompt cache, citing Manus's ~10× cached-vs-uncached differential. That reasoning is sound and I would keep the recommendation.

*Don't Break the Cache: An Evaluation of Prompt Caching for Long-Horizon Agentic Tasks* ([arXiv 2601.06007](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.06007)) sharpens it into something more actionable and more pessimistic. Evaluating across OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google in multi-step agentic settings, it finds:

- Cache hits **frequently fail to occur even when context is reused identically**.
- Invalidation patterns differ **per provider**, and some are driven by internal processing steps not visible to the developer.
- Minor formatting, whitespace, and metadata variation invalidates cached segments.
- Cache boundaries do not reliably align with semantic content units.

The design consequence goes beyond "put retrieved content late." It is: **do not build an architecture whose economics depend on a cache hit rate you cannot verify.** The recommendation set — standardize serialization, separate static from dynamic content, and *measure* hit rate rather than assuming it — should be a Phase 1 instrumentation requirement, not a design assumption.

This also gives a cleaner argument for `COUNTERPOINTS`' central proposal than the one it makes. Tool-call-based pull is not merely cheaper than prefix injection; it is **cheaper in a way that survives provider-side cache behavior you do not control**, because appended tool results are structurally append-only. That is a robustness argument, not just a cost argument, and it is stronger.

## 9. Two questions neither document asks

### 9.1 Should this be built at all, versus adopted?

`RESEARCH.md` Appendix B asks ten questions. `COUNTERPOINTS` §15 adds six. **None of the sixteen is "should we build this, or adopt something and contribute?"**

The prior art is not thin. `COUNTERPOINTS` §6 cites Basic Memory. There is also **memweave** ([Towards Data Science writeup](https://towardsdatascience.com/memweave-zero-infra-ai-agent-memory-with-markdown-and-sqlite-no-vector-database-required/)), whose stated design — *"Markdown files as the source of truth and SQLite as a derived index that's always rebuildable"* — is `COUNTERPOINTS` §6's recommendation, already implemented. Letta, Mem0, Zep, and Cognee occupy the heavier end. OSS agent-memory projects reportedly passed 80,000 aggregate GitHub stars by Q1 2026.

Two honest reasons to build anyway, both of which I find sufficient: (a) the parts you actually care about — evidence links, forgetting, cross-harness portability — are precisely the parts every existing system treats as an afterthought; (b) dependency risk. *Why Memory Components Fail: Eight Years of License and Sustainability Events in Open-Source Data Infrastructure* ([arXiv 2606.24896](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.24896)) catalogues license changes and abandonment in exactly this dependency class. For something meant to outlive several model generations, that argues for a small dependency surface — SQLite, ripgrep, markdown — which is `COUNTERPOINTS`' Phase 0.5 on stronger grounds than it gave.

But the question deserves a written answer in `docs/decisions/`, and right now it has not been asked.

### 9.2 What is the differentiated value, given that the harness now ships most of this?

Claude Code already provides file-based memory with YAML front matter, a loaded index file, on-demand topic files, skills as procedural memory, and MCP. `RESEARCH.md` §4.2 describes this accurately and then designs a system that substantially reimplements it.

Meanwhile the harness layer is *moving*: OWASP's May 2026 piece notes Claude Code v2.1.50 removed user memories from the system prompt specifically to close a high-trust override path ([OWASP](https://genai.owasp.org/2026/05/13/memory-is-a-feature-it-is-also-an-attack-surface/)). That is a vendor changing memory semantics for security reasons, mid-flight. Building five harness adapters (`RESEARCH.md` §13.3) against targets that move like that is a maintenance treadmill — `COUNTERPOINTS` §10 says this and is right.

**The differentiated value of this project is three things, and storage is not one of them:**

1. **Evidence** — every claim traceable to a source span. No harness does this.
2. **Forgetting** — deletion that propagates to derived data and survives backup restore. `RESEARCH.md` §14.5 is genuinely excellent here and is the best section in either document.
3. **Portability** — the store outlives any harness, model, or vendor.

I would scope the project to those three and *deliberately not rebuild* what harnesses ship. Write that down as a non-goal.

## 10. Where I think `RESEARCH-COUNTERPOINTS.md` is wrong

### 10.1 Git is not a substitute for the memory model

`COUNTERPOINTS` §6 presents a table showing git supplies immutable revisions, transaction time, supersession chains, content hashes, audit, rollback, and review — for free. Most of that table is correct. Two rows are not, and the errors are consequential.

**Git versions content, not claims.** `git blame` tells you when a *line changed*. It cannot tell you when a *fact stopped being true*. Valid-time — the entire point of `RESEARCH.md` §8.4 — is orthogonal to commit time and cannot be derived from it. A commit made today can record a fact that became true last March and false last June. The table's "transaction time → commit timestamp" row is right; there is no row for valid time, because git has no answer for it.

**Git makes forgetting maximally hard, and forgetting is a stated requirement.** `RESEARCH.md` §1 requires the system to "forget information completely when requested," and §14.5 specifies deletion that resists backup resurrection. Git history is *designed* to be immutable. Purging a leaked secret or a retracted memory from git means history rewriting, force-push, and re-cloning everywhere the repo exists — the single hardest operation in the tool. `COUNTERPOINTS` proposes git as the audit log and separately endorses §14.5's deletion requirements without noticing they are in direct conflict.

This does not sink the markdown-canonical proposal. It scopes it: **git is a good store for curated, human-authored, non-sensitive knowledge, and a bad store for anything you may later be required to erase.** Which is, notably, exactly the line `RESEARCH.md` §7.1 already draws. On this specific point `RESEARCH.md` is right and `COUNTERPOINTS` is arguing past it.

### 10.2 The grep-beats-embeddings evidence has been contested since that document was written

`COUNTERPOINTS` §2.1 leans on Anthropic removing vector search from Claude Code, plus Manus and Letta, and states the conclusion held by "four teams with strong incentives." §2.2 then honestly concedes that code is an unusually favorable corpus for grep. That concession is correct and, I think, larger than the document allows.

Since then: **ContextBench**, presented by Turbopuffer, tested 50 tasks that deliberately *avoid* naming files or functions — i.e. the cases where grep has no target:

| Approach | Wasted file reads |
|---|---|
| Baseline Claude Code | 1 in 3 |
| Grep alone | 1 in 5 |
| Grep + semantic search | **1 in 8** |

Recall was comparable across grep and semantic; the gain was **precision** ([writeup](https://www.startuphub.ai/ai-news/ai-research/2026/claude-code-benchmarking-semantic-search-vs-grep)).

**Caveat, stated plainly: Turbopuffer sells vector search.** This is vendor-reported and should be discounted exactly as `COUNTERPOINTS` discounts Letta's LoCoMo numbers. But it is a measurement in the direction opposite the document's thesis, on the specific task shape the document identifies as grep's weakness, and it should be in the record.

The synthesis I would actually defend — and which `COUNTERPOINTS` §2.2 point 2 states correctly before contradicting it in §13 — is:

> **The agentic loop is the right control flow. Hybrid retrieval is the right implementation of the tool the loop calls.** These are independent decisions and the document conflates them in its own proposal.

`COUNTERPOINTS` §13's Phase 0.5 specifies "`brain search` = ripgrep with a scope filter." That is not a loop-versus-pipeline choice, it is a lexical-versus-hybrid choice, smuggled in — the exact conflation §2.2 warns against. The correct Phase 0.5 is: agentic loop, tool called iteratively, *implementation of `brain search` deliberately deferred and swappable behind the tool boundary*. That costs nothing extra to build and preserves the decision.

For what it is worth, the general retrieval evidence has not moved: BM25 remains a strong baseline, dense retrieval remains complementary rather than superior, and RRF fusion of the two beats either alone on standard measures. Nothing in 2026 overturned BEIR.

### 10.3 "Drop the confidence floats" is right but incomplete

`COUNTERPOINTS` §7.1 argues for replacing LLM-emitted confidence scalars with a categorical provenance class. I agree entirely — poorly-calibrated floats used as ranking signals multiply false precision into retrieval order, and a `0.92` cannot be audited by anyone.

The incompleteness: a categorical class is only auditable if the **precedence ordering over the categories is written down**. `direct-user-statement > inferred-from-behavior > third-party-document > agent-speculation` reads as obvious and is not. A third-party document from an authoritative source frequently should outrank a user's offhand statement from eight months ago. Without a written table, you have replaced an unauditable float with an unauditable implicit ordering — a real improvement in inspectability, but not the resolution the section claims. See §7.1 above; this is the same precedence policy and should be one artifact.

## 12. Source quality, stated honestly

The user's brief was to avoid leaning on big-name vendors. Both prior documents have a problem here that I want to be explicit about for my own sources too.

**Strong** — independent, methodologically transparent, and load-bearing in my argument:
- Penfield Labs' LoCoMo audit. I independently verified the numbers: 99 score-corrupting errors in 1,540 questions (6.4%), judge accepting 62.81% of deliberately-wrong-but-topical answers. `COUNTERPOINTS` cited this accurately.
- MemDelta ([arXiv 2606.29914](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29914)). Verified. Its specific findings are stronger than `COUNTERPOINTS` conveyed: swapping *only* the embedding model shifts accuracy 6.2pp (p = 0.004), and agent self-memory (42%) **underperforms basic retrieval** (47%).
- ACE ([arXiv 2510.04618](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04618)). Established, multi-institution, with public follow-on work.
- The BM25/hybrid/RRF retrieval literature. Unchanged and well-replicated.

**Directional but weak** — I am relying on these and flagging that they are single-group unreplicated preprints:
- `Ground Truth First` (tenure crossover). n = 6 synthetic users, p = 0.031, synthetic corpus scored by its own authors. **My §1 rests on this.** If it fails to replicate, §1 weakens to "instrument for degradation anyway," which I would still defend on general grounds.
- MEMPROBE, A-TMA, TOKI. All 2026 preprints, none replicated. Useful for problem *framing*; I have not relied on their numbers.

**Vendor-reported, discounted accordingly:**
- ContextBench (Turbopuffer sells vector search).
- Zep's LongMemEval figures, Letta's LoCoMo figures, Mem0's numbers. The [Benchmark Theatre analysis](https://essays.bloo-mind.ai/posts/2026-05-20-mem-eval/) documents the pattern: Zep 84% → corrected to 58.44%; EverMemOS claimed 92.32% versus 38.38% on third-party reproduction; MemPalace's "100% perfect score" achieved by teaching to three specific questions. Its conclusion — ignore published leaderboards, establish full-context and filesystem baselines on *your* data, require 10+ point gains to justify adoption — is the correct operating posture and matches `COUNTERPOINTS` §5.

**A note on `RESEARCH.md` §4.** `COUNTERPOINTS` §0 item 4 objects that seven subsections of vendor documentation summary is against the brief. I agree it is against the brief. I would add that it is not *useless* — §4.3's ADK scope taxonomy and §4.6's checkpointer/store distinction are genuinely clarifying — but it is 40% of a document, it will be stale in two quarters, and the one place it drove a recommendation (the §12.4 MySQL analysis, ~1,000 words answering a question nobody asked) is the weakest section in the document. Compress it to a page of converged conclusions and cite the docs by link.
